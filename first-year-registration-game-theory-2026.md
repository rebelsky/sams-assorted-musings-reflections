Our new registration process for first-year students went live today. For those who don't know, our new first-year registration process is something like the following.

* At 10am on Tuesday, registration opens for first-years to register for one course (plus associated lab, I think). Registration is done on a first-come, first-served basis. It looks like varsity sports practice is scheduled for 9--11am; I guess we don't care about sportos [1] getting their first choice.
* At 2pm on Tuesday, registration opens for first-years to register for their remaining courses (up to 18 credit). Once again, registration is first-come, first-served.

It seems like registration could be an interesting exercise in game theory. After repeatedly refreshing starting at 9:59am, you quickly click the button to register for the course you want that you think is most likely to fill. Then you wait fifteen minutes or so to see where things stand. If that class is far from full, you look to see whether any of your other preferred classes are close to capacity and, if so, you switch your registration. Wash, rinse, and repeat.

Of course, that may not be the optimal strategy, which is why I referred to it as "an interesting exercise in game theory". Are there other ways to game the system? I'm not sure. I'll let the economist think about it.

I discussed this with some colleagues, and they generally suggested that few students are going to spend that much effort. Most will focus on getting one of their top courses and then be satisfied with that. That leads to the other question: What do you put in round one and what do you leave for subsequent rounds? I know that there was some hypothetical research about that, based on the old yellow cards. However, that research is not public. Hence, I'm going to do my on research based on public data.

As you may recall, [I made a list of courses open to first-years and the number of seats available](first-year-courses-2026). Let's see where things stand at about 12:30 p.m. on Tuesday.

_Sam gathers a lot of data._

Welp, that was interesting. I don't think it's worth sharing the individul changes in course enrollments. I've come to realize that one of the most important questions is not so much what fills in round one as it is what fills in round two. If a course you really want is likely to fill in round two, you may need to prioritize it in round one.

However, I still saw some things that surprised me or that I'd like to share from round one.

Most importantly, BIO-150, _Introduction to Biological Inquiry_, completely filled, even with 81 spaces available. All four sections even have waiting lists. Surprisingly, sections 2 and 3 of CHM-129, _General Chemistry_, did not fill, even after round 2. Did no one tell these students that if they are interested in Biology, they'll also need to take CHM-129 in their first year?  Section 1 of CHM-129 filled in the first round. Perhaps that's because students are interested in taking workshop-style chemistry. Perhaps they like 8:00 a.m. classes. Or perhaps it's the instructor, as one student suggested to me.

There were a few multi-section courses in which one or two sections filled in the first round. Again, it might have been an issue of time or a particularly beloved instructor.

The first-round pressure on MAT-215, _Linear Algebra_, looks like it was also pretty high. Unfortunately, I seem to have recorded my original data for MAT-215 incorrectly, so I can't be sure.  Two of the three sections completely filled n round two, so future first-years might want to prioritize MAT-215 in round one.

What about my course, CSC-151, _Functional Problem Solving_? We had 56 spots available before round one. We had 19 spots available after round one. We had two spots available after round two (or at least late into round two).

Okay. On to round two. This time, I'll show you the data.
