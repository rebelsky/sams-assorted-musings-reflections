The class of 2030 will soon be on campus [1]. And in about a week, they will register for their courses.

Why didn't they register for courses over the summer? Because the faculty, as a whole, believe the conversations with their advisors about the liberal arts are an essential part of the registration process. How can we say that we have an "Individually Advised Curriculum" if individual advising isn't part of the process? [2]

In any case, I thought it would be useful to see what courses are currently available to incoming first years and reflect a bit on demand.

I heard that we have about 460 incoming first-year students. If each of them enrolls in three four-credit courses beyond Tutorial, we'll need about 1,380 slots. Ideally, we'll have more slots than that available, since we want student (and advisor) agency in students getting classes. Let's see what four-credit courses are available [3,4,5].

I've grouped the courses according to my somewhat-arbitrary classifications. I've also skipped some courses available to first years, such as CHI-461, _Classical Chinese_, and CSC-161, _Imperative Problem Solving_, because I assume the audience for those courses is small. Aren't assumptions wonderful?

I also realize that courses within a category are not necessarily equivalent. For example, a student who wants to continue in their study of calculus is unlikely to want a course in computer science, a student who wants to explore acting is unlikely to want a course in drawing, and a student interested in intermediate Arabic is unlikely to want to take introductory classical Greek. Still, the numbers provide a broad sense of availability.

Let's see ... 81 + 244 + 176 + 444 + 368 + 302 + 489 = 2,104 slots available. And I claimed we needed about 1,380. It seems like that gives us an appropriate amount of slack in the system. We will face a difficult situation if everyone wants to take, say, ADS-195, BIO-150, CHI-195, DST-247, EDU-101, FMS-155, GRE-101, or HUM-101. But we've generally found that things work out okay, especially since most students understand the value of exploring a bit beyond their top course or two. I worry a little bit that not every first-year student can take an experimental science course this fall, and I worry even more that only one-sixth can take a course in the fine arts. However, not every student wants experimental science in their first semester and, unfortunately, the arts no longer have the same priority in the minds of liberal arts students they once had. There are also alternatives; students interested in the experimental sciences might make do with CS, statistics, or even a social science for the time being and students interested in the fine arts might consider art history, some of the two-credit courses (e.g., two of the two-credit dance courses), or even some of the one-credit performance groups. Nothing is perfect.

I've already had a colleague ask me about how I'd advise my tutorial students to handle the new first-year registration system [9] and what they'd prioritize. A few years ago, it was clear that you had to make CS your first choice if you wanted to study CS in your first semester; that doesn't seem to be the case anymore. It looks to me like there are enough slots between BIO-150 and CHM-129 that a student planning to major in Biology, Biological Chemistry, or Chemistry should be okay, but should still try to get their desired course in the first round [10]. I expect the Studio Art courses (ART-111, _Introduction to the Studio_, and ART-134, _Drawing_) to fill relatively quickly. If I recall correctly, our Philosophy courses fill relatively quickly, so those are also worth considering in the first round. And I know that recent studies have shown that ECN-111 is the course that appeared most on yellow cards [11,12].

For students planning to continue their study of mathematics, it's worth prioritizing math in the second round (perhaps even the first), particularly since there are fewer math openings than first-year students. If I recall correctly---and if things haven't changed---the fall version of MAT-133, _Calculus II_, is particularly designed for students coming from high-school calculus or AP Calculus, while the spring section is designed for students coming out of our MAT-131, _Calculus I_.

More broadly, I think it will be important for students to have fallback courses and fallback plans. Unfortunately, scheduling can mean that if you don't get one course you've planned on, you may need to shift others. Having some detail on the different pathways and a reasonable understanding of how to navigate our registration system will be valuable. That's right! Having all of your courses and the options entered and approved on Self Service [14] will be essential.

The first semester can be complicated. On one hand, you want students to explore broadly. On the other, it's valuable to continue certain subjects. Historically, I've encouraged my students to continue their language study ("Now that you know the language, you get to study the cool stuff, like culture and literature; and you'll start to forget as time goes on") and their math study (ditto, substituting "proof" for "culture and literature"), and then one course in a prospective major.  I know other faculty prefer the "one in each division" approach; I find that problematic because divisions can represent significantly different ways of knowing. I'd like to tell students to try something they've never studied before. Unfortunately, given the limits on the number of courses, that may have to wait until spring.

In any case, good luck to our new students. Don't worry! It's okay if you don't get all of your top choices; part of a liberal education involves exploring new and unexpected topics.
