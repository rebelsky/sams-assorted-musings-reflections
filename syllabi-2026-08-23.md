I've been thinking a lot about course syllabi of late. In part, that's because fall semester will soon be upon us, and I have to get my course syllabi ready. In part, that's because I was scheduled to be part of the IPOP [1] Academic Success discussion, and course syllabi were a core part of the discussion.

For those who aren't part of academia, a course syllabus is a document that lays out information about the course. Most typically, a syllabus contains both course policies and a schedule for the course. The schedule has the anticipated topics for each class as well as due dates for work. Of course, "syllabus" seems to mean "list", so one could argue that the list of topics and work could suffice as a syllabus. Nonetheless, most folks use "syllabus" to mean the broader collection of policies and schedule [2]. 

Unfortunately, students often don't pay enough attention to their syllabi [3]. That is, many don't read through them to make sure they understand course policies, and they don't refer to them when they have questions about policies or due dates for work. Few use the schedules as faculty hope, carefully copying due dates to their own calendars, and regularly referring to the schedules to determine what topics are being covered.

In any case, we were asked to talk about syllabi and to bring syllabi to our meeting with the students. I chose not to bring my syllabi, which are notably long. A colleague had suggested that I look for some Tutorial [5] syllabi. One of the first I found was a recommended one from a 2002 Tutorial. That syllabus had *no* policies, only the list of topics and work. It was only three pages. One from 2016 had three pages of policies and descriptive material (e.g., a description of the course and of Tutorial) and six pages of the schedule. "Modern" syllabi are much longer, at least if we follow recommendations. Why? Well, we are strongly encouraged to include policies about accommodations (including physical and religious), required Title IX text, notes about resources (e.g., libraries, the Math Lab, and the Writing, Reading, Speaking, Presentation, and Other Forms of Communication Center [6]), AI and academic honesty guidelines, and more. Once you gather all of that, syllabi can become somewhat overwhelming.

Let's see what's in my [most recent syllabus for CSC-151](https://rebelsky.cs.grinnell.edu/Courses/CSC151/2025Sp/syllabus/)

* _Introductory information_. This section includes the meeting times, my office hours [8] and office location, the names of our mentors, and times for mentor sessions and CS tutors.
* _About this course_. A short description of the course.
* _Overview_. A longer description of the course.
  * _Introduction_. A short statement of the goals.
  * _Problem domain_. A discussion of the problem domain we explore in the course. (For that course, it was image making.)
  * _Why take CSC-151?_ Reasons to take the course.
  * _Important notices_.  Things we want students to know.
* _Time and Workload_. A discussion of how students should be spending the twelve hours or so allocated to the course [9].
* _Diversity and inclusion_. Lots of issues.
  * _Learning needs_. Mostly a statement about accommodations and adjustments [10] for students with disabilities.
  * _Religious observance policy_. Your faith has priority.
  * _Community guidelines_. We are a community. Respect each other.
  * _Title IX and pregnancy-related conditions_. Text the College requires us to include, along with additional comments.
  * _Basic needs security_. You don't learn as well if your basic needs aren't taken care of.
  * _Other accommodations and adjustments_. The policies above don't cover anything.
  * _Title IX_. I hope that you don't experience Title IX issues. If you disclose assault to me, I must report it. Let me know how I can support you. (I include this both so that students know that I'm a required reporter and to reinforce that I try to be supportive.)
* _Evaluation and Grading_. How do I determine what grade a student has earned?
  * _Deliverables_. The kinds of work I expect: Reading problems, lab exercises, mini-projects, a group project, learning assessments, and metacognitive reflections.
  * _Grading scale and policies_. How I turn your individual grades into a letter grade.
  * _Plus/minus grades_. More complexity to the grading scale.
  * _Reading problems, laboratory assignments, and metacognitive reflections._ How these contribute to the grade.
  * _Redos_. What to do if you don't get the grade you want on a piece of work. For learning assessments: Try again when next offered. For projects: Pay a token and try again.
  * _Final deadline_. 5:00 p.m. on Friday of finals week.
  * _Tokens_. The currency of the course. Spend tokens to redo things. Earn tokens by attending designated events.
  * _Course breakpoints_. A note that I'll submit an Academic Alert if you seem to have fallen behind. I should probably title this "Academic Alerts".
* _Software, textbooks, references, technology, and such_. "Textbooks and readings", for a CS course.
  * _Installing DrRacket_. Instructions for installing the course software.
* _Help, collaboration, academic honesty, and academic integrity_. "To help expedite your learning, you can rely on me, our class mentor, and your peers as resources in this course."
  * _The instructor, course staff, and similar resources_. Me, the mentors, the evening tutors. Also a bit about mentor sessions.
  * _Peer learning_. We generally let you talk to other people about the work you are doing. The one exception is learning assessments.
  * _Sharing of course materials_. Please don't.
  * _AI programming assistants_. Don't use them. 
  * _AI writing assistants_. Cite them if you use them.
* _Other issues_. Because I haven't written enough.
  * _Asking questions_. Questions are good. Ask them on Teams.
  * _Technology_. It will fail.
  * _Mastery grading_. More about the grading approach.
  * _Multiple sections_. I'm teaching three of them. They will (mostly) stay synchronized.
  * _Names_. I screw up. A lot.
  * _Teaching is performative_. A disclaimer?
  * _Feeling overwhelmed_. I'm overwhelmed at this point. It's okay if you are, too.
* _FAQ_. Because every document needs an FAQ [11].

That's a lot, isn't it? When printed, it's about eight pages. However, the font I get seems moderately small, perhaps 10pt. Amazingly, I've even moved more details about some topics (e.g., academic honesty, AI, mastery grading) to separate documents.

As I started this musing, I had expected to use it, in part, to employ some of Cate Denial's ideas from _A Pedagogy of Kindness_ with regard to making a syllabus friendlier. Mine is, to put it politely, a wall of text [14]. While I think it's worth rethinking the text, I'm not sure that her model of rearranging syllabi, which includes adding friendly icons, really matches who I am and how I teach. And there are a lot of icons to find. As I wrote the bullet points above, I realized that they would make a nice "summary syllabus". I think I'm going to try that.

_Time passes. Many days._

Okay. I'm done. I spent about a dozen hours rewriting my syllabus.

You can compare [my new syllabus for CSC-151](https://rebelsky.cs.grinnell.edu/Courses/CSC151/2026Fa/syllabus/) to [my syllabus from two years ago](https://rebelsky.cs.grinnell.edu/Courses/CSC151/2025Sp/syllabus/). 

I tried to make it friendlier. In part, that involved adding the detailed table of contents (similar to what I had above). In part, that involved calling out topics that did not have explicit sections, such as the Final Exam (it doesn't exist) and attendance policies (let me know if you're going to miss class). In part, it involved making the learning objectives explicit. In part, it involved clarifying the many support structures and putting them early in the syllabus. My language has always been moderately supportive, so I didn't feel like I needed to change much there.

I'm not sure that it's within my skill set to incorporate pictures, as Cate Denial does. I'm also not sure that I want my syllabus any longer; with these changes, it's up to 12 pages and about 10,000 words [15]. The Interweb tells me that a slow reader or someone reading aloud should be able to read it in an hour.

Of course, I also changed things that don't necessarily make the syllabus kinder, but that I felt were appropriate to update. I've switched from weekly projects to bi-weekly projects. I switched project grading from "Excellent, Meets Expectations, Redo, Incomplete, Missing" to "Satisfactory or Not-Yet-Satisfactory". Because AI now rules out take-home exams, I added in-class time for weekly quizzes, which will be the primary assessment. I also cut the number of learning objectives from twenty-four down to twenty so that there's a bit more slack [16].

What's the point of all this? I've been intending to make my syllabi and my course kinder. My policies have been kind. I think they're kinder now. My syllabus was a wall of text. It still is. But it has a nice one-page introductory table of contents that lays out the key ideas. I think that's kinder. I'm counting it as a success, at least if I ignore the time involved.

What's next? That's right, updating the schedule, writing the assignments, getting the software to work, setting up Teams and Gradescope, creating accounts for the students (which has to wait until students finish enrolling), and many other things I've forgotten. Yay!
