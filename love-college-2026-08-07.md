I was in a workshop the other day, and, at some point, everyone was asked to discuss their relationship to the College and how it has changed. When it was my turn, I said something like "I love the College, but I've come to learn that the College doesn't love me." 

During a break, a colleague said something like, "What do you mean the College doesn't love you. So many people love you: students, alums, the people in my department, and many more." I said that I understood that; many people seem to think fondly of me, even though I can be a pain in the ass. In fact, someone later suggested that some people think fondly of me because I'm a pain the ass. "People appreciate that you're willing to speak your mind." [1]

I struggled to explain why, in spite of knowing that lots of people at the College love me or think fondly of me, I can say "The College doesn't love me." I know that to be true, but why? My muse, in her infinite wisdom, suggested I might figure out an answer by writing about it. Here goes.

Perhaps I should start with why I love the College. Core to that affection is my experience that Grinnell provides a top-notch small-classroom liberal education to a large number of students who could not otherwise afford such an education. Grinnell has a bunch of money and, at least from that perspective, uses some of it for this important purpose. There are, of course, reasonable debates as to whether our current approach [2] is the best. For example, some schools set income bars below which students and their families pay no tuition, or maybe even less than that. Nonetheless, core to Grinnell's mission is providing an excellent education to students who would not otherwise have the resources for such an eduction.

I also love our students, or at least most of our students. By and large, they are smart, committed to learning, interested in a wide variety of subjects, and enthusiastic about making a positive difference in the world. As I've said before [4], Grinnell has great students, and I'm privileged to be able to make a positive difference in some of their lives.

I love my colleagues and their commitment to providing an excellent education. So many of them are creative educators and researchers!

I also love our staff and administrators, even though I sometimes have conflicts with them. In my experience, they place the success of our students at the center of their work.

As I said, many of them feel similarly about me. That is, they find that I care deeply about our students, am creative in my approach to teaching, and make a positive difference to those students.

So why don't I think the College loves me? In part, when I think of "the College", it's as a non-thinking entity that doesn't really love anyone. I realize that an institution should be made up of the people within it, but my experience is that the combination and intersection of decisions and choices that well-meaning people make do not always have positive impact.

Strike that.

My experience is that these decisions and choices can have a significantly negative impact, even if we don't intend it as such.

For example, during the pandemic, the College further prioritized security of FERPA-protected data. It is certainly worth keeping such data secure. But the approaches were unfriendly, particularly to people in CS. "You can't use software we haven't explicitly approved" suggests that you don't trust us to make good decisions. Why is it that we need approval for the software we use for teaching, but not our textbooks? "You can't recommend software that we haven't approved" violates my academic freedom and suggests that you don't respect my professional expertise. "We have to review any software you plan to distribute outside the College" interferes with my and my colleagues ability to share our research. Does the College insist on reviewing the papers and poems faculty write? No. So why our software?

When I complained about the policies, I got surprising pushback. I was able to get the software recommendation and software distribution policies revoked, but it took significant effort. It took a few more years until the faculty got more trust in their software decisions, although there's still some mistrust in the system.

Love includes trust. When the institution says that it doesn't trust my decisions, it undermines that love. When it makes bad policies and bad decisions, it shows something other than love. And, even when it corrects those bad policies and bad decisions, if it doesn't apologize, it fails to act lovingly.

I also don't think the College treats many (most?) of our staff with love. I won't attempt to explain all the reasons, at least as I understand them, but compensation (more precisely, lack thereof) is certainly one reason.

I wrote a few more paragraphs about frustrations. I don't think they added anything to the musing. And I'm still not sure I completely---or even partially---answered the question.
